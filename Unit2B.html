Key terms

Multilateralism
Coordinating action with multiple states, usually through an international body or coalition, so decisions carry broad legitimacy and the costs and risks are shared. It tends to take longer, since it requires building consensus.
Unilateralism
A state acting on its own judgment, using its own resources, without waiting for international authorization or a broad coalition. It can be faster and unconstrained, but it carries the full cost and risk alone and can damage relationships with other states.
Mutual defense clause
A treaty provision where members agree that an attack on one is treated as an attack on all. Invoking it is a way of turning a bilateral attack into a multilateral response.
Coalition of the willing
An ad hoc group of states that join a military or political effort without a formal mandate from a body like the UN Security Council. It sits between full multilateralism and true unilateralism.
Security Council authorization
Formal approval from the UN Security Council for the use of force. It is the clearest source of international legal legitimacy for military action, but any of the five permanent members can block it with a veto.
Legitimacy
Whether other states and institutions view an action as rightful and lawful, rather than merely powerful. Legitimacy affects how much cooperation a state can expect afterward.
Burden-sharing
Spreading the financial, military, and political costs of an intervention across multiple partners, rather than one state bearing them alone.
