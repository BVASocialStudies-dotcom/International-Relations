Read together, roughly 85–90% of respondents favor some form of the status quo or eventual independence, while combined support for any form of unification with the PRC has stayed in the single digits for years. A separate, differently-worded poll by the Taiwan Public Opinion Foundation (TPOF) finds even higher independence support (around 44%) and lower "don't know" — the gap between polls comes down to how the options are framed, not a real disagreement about the underlying trend: unification support is small and has been shrinking for three decades, while identification as "Taiwanese only" (rather than "Chinese" or "both") has risen for the same span.

China, Japan & South Korea

Two very different trajectories in 2026: a historic diplomatic collapse with Japan, and a deliberate warming with South Korea.

Relationship in crisis

Japan

Japan and China maintain deep trade ties but a long-standing rivalry over the East China Sea's Senkaku/Diaoyu Islands (administered by Japan, claimed by China) and historical grievances from Japan's 20th-century occupation of parts of China.

Relations collapsed after newly-installed Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi told parliament on November 7, 2025 that a Chinese attack on Taiwan involving warships could pose an "existential threat" to Japan — justifying a Japanese military response. Beijing called this an unprecedented break with Japan's traditional caution on Taiwan and demanded a retraction.

China responded with a sweeping pressure campaign: a ban on Japanese seafood imports, suspended tourism, expanded export controls on dual-use goods, and a downgrade of cultural exchanges. Japan's 2026 Diplomatic Bluebook downgraded China's official status from "one of the most important neighboring countries" to simply "an important neighbor." A cross-party Japanese delegation traveled to Beijing in August 2026 to try to repair ties, with limited success so far.

Relationship warming

South Korea

China is South Korea's largest trading partner, but relations have been shadowed since 2016 by Seoul's deployment of the U.S. THAAD missile-defense system, which Beijing sees as a threat to its own security despite South Korean and U.S. assurances it targets only North Korea. China retaliated economically at the time, hitting South Korean retail and entertainment exports.

Under President Lee Jae-myung, ties have warmed noticeably: Lee met Xi Jinping in Gyeongju in October 2025, then made a state visit to Beijing in January 2026 for a second summit just two months later — an unusually fast pace signaling both sides want smoother relations, partly to keep channels open on North Korea's nuclear program.

China's Military Buildup

Data and charts adapted from Matthew P. Funaiole and Brian Hart, "China's Military Buildup," Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS). Click each item to expand it.

Constant 2023 U.S. dollars. Source: SIPRI · Chart adapted from Fabio Murgia/CSIS.

China's rise as an economic power has fueled three decades of steady military spending growth. Its official 2025 defense budget reached nearly $247 billion, but that figure understates real spending: SIPRI estimates China's actual 2024 defense spending at about $318 billion, while another study puts it as high as $471 billion. Whatever the precise number, the scale and pace of growth — roughly a 13-fold increase since the mid-1990s — have funded broad investment in equipment, maintenance, personnel, and training.

China still spends significantly less on defense than the United States, but the gap is narrowing. In 2012, China's defense budget was about one-sixth of the U.S. figure; by 2024 it had grown to roughly one-third. Regionally the picture looks very different: China now outspends Japan on defense by about five times, and South Korea by nearly seven times — both key U.S. allies in the Indo-Pacific.

Battle force ship counts, approximate; dashed lines are projections to 2030. Source: CRS; DOD · Chart adapted from Fabio Murgia/CSIS.

Nowhere is the PLA's progress more visible than in its navy, which has evolved from a coastal defense force into a "blue-water navy" capable of projecting power globally. The PLA Navy overtook the U.S. Navy in number of battle force ships around 2014 and is projected to keep growing — by roughly 48% more ships by 2030, according to U.S. estimates. China still trails the U.S. in overall tonnage, on-ship missile capacity, and far-seas experience, but the Pentagon assesses that China's ability to operate beyond the "first island chain" is "modest but growing" as the PLA gains more experience.

China's air forces have modernized alongside its navy. The PLA Air Force and Navy together now field a much larger fleet of combat aircraft than in years past, and — more importantly — a growing share of that fleet is technologically advanced. Older second- and third-generation fighters are steadily being retired in favor of 4.5-generation and fifth-generation aircraft, including the stealthy, precision-strike-capable J-20.

China's improving capabilities have significantly shifted the military balance in the Taiwan Strait. Xi Jinping has ordered the PLA to be capable of seizing Taiwan by force by 2027, and Taiwan remains the PLA's top priority. A large share of PLA assets — especially naval and air assets — are concentrated in the Eastern and Southern Theater Commands, which would be most directly involved in any conflict over Taiwan.

Nuclear warhead counts, approximate; dashed line is the DOD's projection to 2035. Source: FAS; DOD · Chart adapted from Fabio Murgia/CSIS.

The growth of China's nuclear arsenal has become one of the most alarming developments for Washington and its allies. By independent estimates, China's stockpile reached about 600 warheads in 2025, more than doubling since 2019. The U.S. Department of Defense forecasts that China will keep expanding its arsenal to roughly 1,500 warheads by 2035 — nearly tripling again over the next decade. That would put China much closer to parity with the United States and Russia, forcing Washington to plan around not one nuclear-armed rival, but two.

Missile launcher counts, approximate. Source: IISS · Chart adapted from Fabio Murgia/CSIS.

Beyond its expanding nuclear stockpile, the PLA Rocket Force has built the world's largest arsenal of ground-based conventional and dual-use missiles. The number of intermediate-range ballistic missiles in particular has grown quickly with the fielding of the DF-26 missile, which has an estimated range of up to 4,000 kilometers — putting distant U.S. military bases like Guam within reach and further complicating the security picture for U.S. allies like Japan.
