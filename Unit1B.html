Technology in International Relations
The AI Race

Artificial intelligence has become a defining arena of U.S.–China competition, with both governments treating AI leadership as a matter of national strategy, not just commercial advantage.

Washington has leaned on private-sector innovation and export controls on advanced chips to preserve its edge, while Beijing has poured state investment into domestic chip production and talent to cut its dependence on foreign technology. Chinese firms have recently narrowed that gap — raising a pointed question for U.S. policymakers: is America's lead as secure as assumed, and if not, what should change?

This assignment is built around the article below, in which Dewardic L. McNeal argues that the U.S. advantage in AI has narrowed dramatically — or disappeared altogether — and makes the case for a new national strategy. As you read, weigh his evidence against the assumptions behind current U.S. policy.

“The U.S. Lead Over China in AI Is All But Gone. We Need to Change Our National Strategy.”

By Dewardic L. McNeal

02 The Current Situation

For years, people debating artificial intelligence asked two main questions: Can U.S. tech companies keep leading the way? And can the U.S. government build a strategy to protect its advantage over China? Those questions don’t matter as much anymore, because recent events have already answered them.

The real question now is different. It is no longer whether China can compete at the highest level — it already can. The question is whether the U.S. can adapt fast enough to keep up with China’s AI industry, which is improving not just how well its models perform, but also their cost, ease of use, financing, global standards, and reach. Washington often reacts to China’s breakthroughs instead of shaping the competition itself. That should worry policymakers, tech leaders, investors, and U.S. allies more than any single benchmark score, because this race is turning into a battle between entire innovation systems, not just individual companies.

News about companies like DeepSeek, Moonshot AI’s Kimi K3, Alibaba’s Qwen models, Tencent’s Hunyuan, and Zhipu AI and MiniMax often get reported as separate stories. They are not. Together, they show something bigger than a handful of successful Chinese AI companies — they show that China has built an entire AI ecosystem capable of producing world-class technology again and again, across many firms at once. It matters less whether these advances come from original ideas, clever engineering, open collaboration, or copying U.S. models. What matters is that the progress is happening across a whole system, while the U.S. still judges each company one at a time, often treating each new breakthrough as a one-time surprise instead of a sign of a bigger shift.

For years, the U.S. has underestimated China’s commitment to long-term technology growth and its skill at turning government strategy into real global advantage. This happened with rare earth minerals, electric vehicles, robotics, semiconductors, and now artificial intelligence. The mistake has been the same each time: Washington tends to judge China’s progress company by company and product by product, often assuming each success is a fluke or won’t last. Meanwhile, Beijing has followed a patient strategy built to help its whole industry innovate and grow at the same time.

It is also important to understand that China’s plan to become a technology superpower started long before the Biden administration’s technology restrictions. Those restrictions may have changed the speed or direction of China’s progress, but they did not create it. DeepSeek’s announcement in January 2025 forced many people to notice a strategy China had already been building for over a decade through industrial policy and national five-year plans. The breakthrough was not the strategy itself — it was proof the strategy was starting to work.

03 The Ecosystem of Statecraft

This bigger strategy is what I call “ecosystem statecraft.” It is a type of competition where a country tries to shape the entire environment where new technology is built, funded, standardized, used, and promoted. It combines industrial policy, financing, innovation, global standards, university programs, developer communities, diplomacy, and business expansion into one clear national strategy aimed at long-term technological leadership. Instead of competing company by company or product by product, ecosystem statecraft tries to shape the conditions that decide which technologies succeed.

Artificial intelligence is just the clearest example of this strategy today. China has used the same approach with semiconductors, electric vehicles, batteries, robotics, telecommunications, renewable energy, critical minerals, digital infrastructure, and advanced manufacturing. AI is not an exception to China’s industrial strategy — it is the most advanced example of it.

Seen this way, the U.S. and China appear to be following very different plans for winning. American policy has focused on staying ahead through new inventions, while also slowing China down with export controls, investment restrictions, and limits on advanced computing power. These tools are still important and should remain part of America’s strategy. China, meanwhile, seems focused on shaping the environment where global tech competition happens. Chinese AI companies are making their technology easier to use, easier to customize, easier to combine with different computing systems, and easier for developers, businesses, and governments worldwide to build on. In the long run, removing these obstacles may matter just as much as having the best-performing model.

04 A Race to “Addict” the Rest of the World

President Xi Jinping’s recent speech at the World Artificial Intelligence Conference reflected this bigger plan. By highlighting international cooperation, government rules, open-source development, and support for developing countries, China positioned itself as more than just an AI producer — it wants to be seen as the designer of an alternative global tech system. At the same time, China is tightening control over its most important technologies at home while pushing other countries to adopt its AI platforms. The strategy is clear: protect key technology at home while expanding influence abroad. U.S. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick even said, during debate over a ban on Nvidia chip exports, that the real goal — echoing Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang — is to make the rest of the world “addicted” to one country’s technology. But it is increasingly unclear that America’s technology is the one winning that race.

This bigger strategy also explains why convincing other countries to avoid Chinese AI will likely be much harder than the earlier U.S. campaign against Huawei and ZTE. Governments can regulate telecom infrastructure fairly easily. But they have far less control over which AI models, software tools, and developer platforms millions of programmers choose to use and build into their own products. More and more, technology spreads from the ground up, not just from decisions made at the top.

At the same time, many governments now judge both the U.S. and China more practically. They balance relationships with both countries, weighing security concerns alongside cost, financing, technical ability, local job creation, and long-term economic benefits. In the end, countries adopt the technology systems of partners they trust to stay reliable, affordable, accessible, and committed for the long haul. Trust, funding, developer communities, shared standards, business partnerships, and diplomatic relationships have all become real advantages in this competition.

What worries me most is not China’s progress — it is how the U.S. talks about its response. Too often, America’s AI conversation focuses on what is good for individual companies instead of what is good for the country’s long-term interests. That is not a criticism of OpenAI, Anthropic, Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon, Google, or any other company — their job is to make money and stay competitive. Government has a different job. Companies aim for competitive advantage. Governments should aim for national advantage. Those goals often line up, but not always.

The U.S. still has major strengths. Its universities remain among the best in the world. Its investment community is unmatched. Its semiconductor industry powers the global AI economy. And its research labs keep producing breakthroughs. But history shows that being first to invent something does not guarantee long-term leadership. What usually matters more is who builds the system that everyone else eventually chooses to join.

So the real question for the U.S. is not just whether American companies can keep building the world’s best AI models. It is whether the U.S. can build a national strategy that lasts beyond changes in political leadership — one that combines innovation with strong alliances, global standards, research, talent development, business partnerships, funding, and renewed trust around the world. Once this becomes a competition between entire systems, success will not just depend on which models perform best. It will depend on which country’s system developers, researchers, entrepreneurs, universities, businesses, and investors choose to trust and build upon. By that measure, the United States still has work to do.
